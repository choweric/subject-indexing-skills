# LCSH Conceptual Analysis Expert

Conceptual analysis is the first phase of subject analysis. It determines a resource's **aboutness** and produces an **aboutness statement** that guides translation into controlled vocabularies (LCSH, classification).

## Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Aboutness** | The subject matter AND genre/form properties of a resource |
| **Is-ness** | What the resource IS (form/genre), not what it's about |
| **Of-ness** | What a visual resource depicts (for images) |
| **Aboutness Statement** | A natural language summary of the resource's aboutness |

## Phase 1: Examine the Resource

Review these bibliographic features systematically:

| Feature | What to Look For |
|---------|------------------|
| Title & subtitle | Topic indicators, but beware of misleading/metaphorical titles |
| Creator information | Author expertise, discipline background |
| Table of contents | Structure, major sections, scope |
| Introduction/Preface | Statement of purpose, intended audience |
| Chapter headings | Subtopics, organization |
| Index terms | Key concepts the author emphasized |
| Abstract/Summary | Author's own description of content |
| Covers/Jackets | Publisher's summary, audience indicators |
| Illustrations/Diagrams | Visual subject matter |

**Caution (H 180 sec. O):** Do not classify by title alone. Apply judgment:
- If the title is **misleading, euphemistic, or cryptic**, do not use it as a guide to contents.
- If the title is **general** but the work is on a more specific topic, focus on the specific topic.
- If many topics are listed on the title page (like a table of contents), treat them as a TOC.
- If the title states the subject clearly, bring out each topic of **subject retrieval value**.

## Phase 2: Apply Conceptual Analysis Methods

Use multiple methods in combination. No single method is sufficient alone.

### Wilson's Four Methods

#### 1. Purposive Method
Identify the **author's intent** in creating the work.

**Questions to ask:**
- What did the creator hope to achieve?
- What is stated in the preface/introduction?
- Does the title reflect the purpose?

**Evidence sources:**
- Direct statements of purpose in preface
- Title (but verify against content)
- Author's background/expertise

**Example:**
> Title: "Women and the First World War"
> Preface states: "This book will explore the many ways in which women experienced the war..."
> Purpose: To describe how WWI affected women globally

#### 2. Figure-Ground Method
Identify the **central figure** or main idea that "stands out."

**Questions to ask:**
- What concept is most emphasized?
- What is the dominant theme?
- What would a reader remember most?

**Caution:** Different catalogers may perceive different central figures. Education, background, and assumptions influence what "stands out."

**Resolution:** If two figures are equally prominent, treat them as coequal subjects.

#### 3. Objective Method
**Count references** to terms and concepts empirically.

**Questions to ask:**
- What terms appear most frequently?
- What concepts are consistently mentioned?

**Challenges:**
- Same concept may use different terms (WWI, World War I, Great War)
- Primary concepts may not be explicitly named
- Dominant term may be background, not focus

**Best practice:** Look at the *context* of references, not just frequency.

#### 4. Cohesion Method
Identify what **unifies** the document and what was **selected or excluded**.

**Questions to ask:**
- What makes this document coherent?
- What rule of selection/rejection explains what's included?
- What was deliberately left out?

**Principle:** Finding a description that explains the presence of most details reveals the true subject.

### Langridge's Three Questions

#### Question 1: What IS the resource?
Identify the **form of knowledge** (how we perceive phenomena):

| Form | Description |
|------|-------------|
| 1. Prolegomena | Instruments of knowledge (logic, math, linguistics) |
| 2. Philosophy | Philosophical inquiry |
| 3. Natural Science | Scientific study of nature |
| 4. Technology | Useful/applied arts |
| 5. Human Science | Behavioral/social sciences |
| 6. Social Practice | Applied social knowledge |
| 7. History | Historical study |
| 8. Moral Knowledge | Ethics, values |
| 9. Religion | Religious texts/study |
| 10. Art | Artistic works/study |
| 11. Criticism | Critical analysis |
| 12. Personal Experience | Memoirs, autobiography |

#### Question 2: What is the resource ABOUT?
Identify the **topics/phenomena** discussed. This is the general subject matter that can be examined through any form of knowledge.

**Note:** A resource may have multiple primary topics. Record all significant ones.

#### Question 3: What is the resource FOR?
Identify the **purpose and intended audience**.

- Why was this created?
- Who is the intended reader?
- Is it directed toward a particular discipline?
- What intellectual level? (introductory, intermediate, advanced)

**Example:**
> A biophysics textbook may be intended for general biology majors, not biophysics majors.
> Topic: biophysics
> Audience: biology students (not biophysics specialists)

### Langridge's Additional Considerations

**Nature of Thought:**
- Point of view/perspective (e.g., agnostic view of spirituality)
- Type of writing (persuasive, expository, instructive, imaginative)
- Methodology
- Intellectual level

**Nature of Text:**
- Medium (language, translation, pictorial)
- Bibliographic form (monograph, anthology, essay)
- Form of presentation

## Phase 3: Identify Concept Types (Joudrey & Taylor)

Systematically identify concepts in these five categories:

### 1. Discipline
The branch of knowledge in which the resource fits.

**Examples:** Medicine, History, Art, Computer Science

### 2. Topics
The principal objects of attention or themes.

**Note:** Be as specific as possible. "Training dogs" is less accurate than "potty training cockapoo puppies."

### 3. Names
All types of named entities:
- Persons
- Corporate bodies
- Geographic regions/features
- Titles of works
- Events
- Buildings
- Products

### 4. Chronological Elements
Time periods that contextualize the content:
- Specific dates or date ranges
- Named historical periods (Industrial Revolution, Renaissance)
- Centuries

### 5. Form and/or Genre
What the resource IS (is-ness):
- Literary genre (satirical fiction, mystery)
- Artistic form (oil painting, documentary)
- Publication format (textbook, encyclopedia, anthology)

**Be specific:** Not just "moving image" but "independent animated film"

## Phase 4: Construct the Aboutness Statement

Compile your notes into a natural language summary.

### Structure
An aboutness statement may be:
- One or two sentences
- A short paragraph
- A bulleted list

### Content
Include:
- Discipline/form of knowledge
- Primary topics
- Significant named entities
- Time period (if relevant)
- Geographic focus (if relevant)
- Form/genre (is-ness)
- Intended audience (if clear)
- Author's purpose

### Example

**Resource:** *Guts 'n' Stuff* (children's book)

| Concept | Data | Source |
|---------|------|--------|
| Author's intent | Overview of major organ systems | Wilson's Purposive |
| Knowledge area | Medicine / Natural Science | Langridge Q1 |
| Genre/Form | Anatomical atlas / Anatomy book | Is-ness |
| Audience | Children / Introductory level | Langridge Q3 |
| Topics | Nervous, circulatory, digestive systems | Langridge Q2 |
| Nature of text | Many illustrations and diagrams | Examination |

**Aboutness Statement:**
> This medical resource is a children's anatomy book about the nervous, circulatory, and digestive organ systems. It aims to provide introductory information on these topics and is supported by many illustrations and diagrams.

## Guiding Principles

### Specificity
Be as specific as the resource allows. Identify the exact level of concepts:
- Not "dogs" but "puppies"
- Not "training" but "potty training"
- Not "cockapoo puppies" if only "puppies" applies

### Exhaustivity
Identify all **potentially significant** concepts at this phase:
- Focus on dominant overall subjects, but also note secondary topics
- Include named entities even if they seem minor — per **H 180 sec. N.3**, headings for named entities (persons, families, corporate bodies, projects, events, buildings, named products, uniform titles) are assigned when they are **critical to the subject of the work as a whole, even if discussion of them does not form 20% of the work** (cf. H 430 for name heading validation, H 405 for entity type classification)
- **Do not pre-filter**: The `lcsh-quantitative-filtering` skill will apply the formal 20% rule with its exceptions (SHM H 180)

### Objectivity (H 180 sec. Q)
Subject analysis is inherently subjective, but LC policy is clear:
- **Avoid assigning headings that label topics or express personal value judgments** regarding topics or materials
- Individual cataloger knowledge and judgment inevitably play a role, but headings should not reflect a cataloger's **opinion** about the contents
- Consider the **intent of the author or publisher** and assign headings for that orientation without being judgmental
- Follow stated intentions regarding readership, audience level, treatment as fact or fiction, etc.

### Independence from Translation
Perform conceptual analysis **before** considering controlled vocabulary.
> "If the cataloger is approaching a text with the intention of fitting the subject matter into their system of vocabulary, the indexer may lose some of the nuances." — Chu & O'Brien

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Solution |
|---------|----------|
| Assuming one "correct" subject | Accept multiple valid interpretations |
| Classifying by title alone | Verify title against actual content |
| Premature filtering | Identify all significant concepts; filter in the next phase |
| Premature translation | Complete conceptual analysis first |
| Ignoring form/genre | Always identify is-ness |
| Missing named entities | Systematically check for all name types |
| Ignoring audience | Consider Langridge's "What is it for?" |
